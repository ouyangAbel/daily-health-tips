Late Summer TCM Wellness: The Fifth Season — Nourish the Spleen, Clear Dampness & Harmonize the Earth Element

📅 August 5, 2026 • Seasonal Wellness
Advertisement Space (728x90)

When most people think of summer, they picture blazing heat, cold drinks, and late sunsets. But in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), there is a fifth season that the Western calendar entirely overlooks: Late Summer (长夏, Cháng Xià). This quiet, humid transition between the fiery peak of summer and the crisp onset of autumn is governed by the Earth element (土, Tǔ) and holds the key to some of the most common chronic health complaints — bloating, fatigue, brain fog, loose stools, and that heavy, sluggish feeling that no amount of coffee can shake. Understanding Late Summer and learning to harmonize with it is one of the most powerful yet overlooked practices in TCM wellness.

Far from being a minor footnote in the seasonal cycle, Late Summer is considered the pivot of the year in TCM — the moment when Yang energy begins its descent back toward Yin, and the body's digestive foundation (the Spleen and Stomach) takes center stage. If you've ever felt mysteriously tired in August, or noticed your digestion worsen during humid weather, you've felt the influence of the Earth element at work. This guide will walk you through everything you need to know: the theory behind TCM's fifth season, how to recognize Dampness and Spleen Qi deficiency, which foods and acupoints can restore balance, and practical daily habits to keep your Earth element grounded and strong.

Key Takeaways

  • TCM recognizes five seasons — Late Summer (长夏) is the fifth, governed by the Earth element, Spleen, and Stomach
  • Late Summer corresponds to humid, transitional weather and is the most critical season for digestive health
  • The Spleen in TCM is the organ of transformation — it converts food into Qi and Blood, and it is the primary target of Dampness (湿, Shī)
  • Seven major signs of Spleen Qi deficiency and Dampness include bloating, loose stools, brain fog, heavy limbs, poor appetite, fatigue after eating, and a thick tongue coating
  • Warming, easily digestible foods — particularly yellow, sweet, and root vegetables — are central to Late Summer eating
  • Key acupoints like SP6 (Sanyinjiao), ST36 (Zusanli), and CV12 (Zhongwan) strengthen the Spleen and resolve Dampness
  • Lifestyle practices — avoiding cold drinks, managing worry, eating at regular times, and gentle afternoon exercise — are just as important as diet

What Is Late Summer? TCM's Fifth Season

In Western culture, we recognize four seasons. Traditional Chinese Medicine, however, has always understood that nature's rhythm is more nuanced. The Huangdi Neijing (The Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine) describes five distinct seasonal phases, each corresponding to one of the Five Elements (五行, Wǔ Xíng): Wood (Spring), Fire (Summer), Earth (Late Summer), Metal (Autumn), and Water (Winter). Late Summer is not merely the last few weeks of August — it is the transitional period between any two seasons, the moment of equilibrium when one phase yields to the next. In practice, it is most often associated with the humid, heavy weeks from late July through mid-September.

Why does TCM carve out this fifth season? Because the Earth element serves as the center — the axis around which the other four elements revolve. Earth does not belong to one moment in the year; it belongs to every moment of transition. It is the soil from which crops grow, the ground beneath your feet, the digestive fire that transforms raw sustenance into human vitality. The Earth element's organ pair — the Spleen (脾, Pí) and Stomach (胃, Wèi) — are collectively called the "Root of the Postnatal Body" (后天之本) because they are responsible for extracting Qi and Blood from everything you eat and drink. If the Earth element is weak, every other organ system suffers — no matter how much Fire (heart) or Water (kidney) you cultivate.

"The Spleen and Stomach are the granary officials of the body. They store the food and transform it into the five flavors — and into the Qi and Blood that sustain all life."

— Huangdi Neijing (黄帝内经), the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon, c. 200 BCE

The Earth Element: Spleen, Stomach, and the Dampness Factor

To understand Late Summer wellness, you must first understand the TCM Spleen — which is not the same as the Western anatomical spleen. In TCM, the Spleen is a functional system responsible for several critical processes:

  • Transformation and Transport (运化, Yùn Huà): The Spleen converts food and drink into usable Qi and Blood, then transports these refined substances to the lungs, heart, and throughout the body. This is arguably the most important metabolic process in TCM.
  • Governing Blood (统血, Tǒng Xuè): The Spleen holds the Blood within the vessels. When Spleen Qi is weak, Blood may escape its channels — manifesting as bruising, heavy menstrual bleeding, or chronic bleeding disorders.
  • Raising the Clear Yang (升清, Shēng Qīng): The Spleen sends pure, refined Qi upward to the brain, senses, and heart. When this function fails, you experience brain fog, poor concentration, and organ prolapse (in severe cases).
  • Controlling the Limbs and Flesh: The Spleen nourishes muscles and maintains their tone. Weak Spleen Qi leads to muscle atrophy, weakness, and a feeling of heaviness in the limbs.

The Spleen's arch-nemesis is Dampness (湿, Shī). Unlike other organs that fear Heat or Cold, the Spleen is uniquely vulnerable to Dampness — a heavy, sticky, pathogenic factor that clogs the body's channels the way wet clay clogs a drain. Late Summer's humid, heavy weather literally invites Dampness into the body. When external Dampness invades, it lodges in the Spleen and disrupts its transforming function. The result: bloating, loose stools, a feeling of heaviness, brain fog, nausea, and a thick, greasy coating on the tongue.

7 Signs Your Earth Element Needs Attention

How do you know if your Spleen is struggling and Dampness is accumulating? TCM practitioners look for a cluster of characteristic signs. If you recognize three or more of the following, your Late Summer wellness practice should begin immediately.

1. Chronic Bloating and Poor Digestion

Food sits heavy in your stomach for hours after eating. You feel distended, gassy, and uncomfortable — especially after consuming raw, cold, or greasy foods. This is a hallmark sign of Spleen Qi deficiency: the digestive fire is too weak to "cook" the food properly.

2. Loose Stools or Irregular Bowel Movements

The Spleen's failure to transform fluids results in Dampness descending to the intestines, causing loose stools, mucus in the stool, or alternating constipation and diarrhea. The stool often feels sticky and difficult to clean — a classic Dampness indicator.

3. Brain Fog and Poor Concentration

When the Spleen fails to raise Clear Yang to the head, the brain becomes "misted" by Dampness. You may feel mentally sluggish, unable to focus, or experience a "fuzzy" feeling in the head that worsens after eating. This is often misdiagnosed as ADHD or chronic fatigue in Western medicine.

4. Heavy Limbs and Physical Fatigue

Dampness is heavy by nature — it sinks and lingers in the lower body. If your legs feel like lead, your arms are heavy to lift, and you feel exhausted by mild exertion, Dampness is likely overwhelming your Spleen's ability to transport fluids.

5. Poor Appetite or Cravings for Sweets

The Earth element naturally craves the sweet flavor — but in a deficient state, this craving becomes pathological. You may have no appetite for real food yet desperately want pastries, candy, or bread. The Spleen is crying out for Qi but seeking it in the wrong form.

6. Thick, Greasy Tongue Coating

In TCM tongue diagnosis, a thick, white or yellow greasy coating on the middle of the tongue (the Spleen/Stomach zone) is a definitive sign of Dampness. The coating looks like fur or moss stuck to the tongue surface — the wetter and greasier, the more severe the Dampness.

7. Emotional Worry and Overthinking

Each element carries an emotional component. The Earth element's emotion is worry (思, Sī) — the tendency to overthink, ruminate, excessive mental work, and obsessive problem-solving. Chronic worry damages the Spleen, creating a vicious cycle: Spleen deficiency generates worry, and worry further weakens the Spleen.

Late Summer Foods: Nourish the Earth with Yellow and Sweet

The Late Summer diet follows a simple principle: eat foods that strengthen the Spleen, resolve Dampness, and avoid anything that further burdens the digestive system. The Earth element's color is yellow, its flavor is sweet, and its foods are typically root vegetables, grains, and legumes that grow in the soil. But "sweet" in TCM does not mean candy — it refers to the natural sweetness found in whole, unprocessed foods that gently tonify Qi.

🥣 Millet (小米, Xiǎo Mǐ) — The Spleen's Best Friend

Millet is the premier Spleen-tonifying grain in TCM dietary therapy. It is warm, sweet, and easily digestible — ideal for rebuilding a weakened digestive system. Cooked into a thin porridge (粥, Zhōu), millet strengthens the Stomach, stops diarrhea, and nourishes Qi without creating Dampness. Unlike wheat (which is cooling and can exacerbate Dampness), millet is gentle and warming. For Late Summer breakfast, a bowl of millet porridge with a few red dates and ginger slices is the perfect Earth-nourishing meal.

🎃 Pumpkin and Squash (南瓜, Nán Guā)

Sweet, warming, and golden — pumpkins and winter squash are quintessential Earth element foods. They tonify the Spleen, harmonize the Stomach, and gently move Qi without being harsh. In TCM, pumpkin is used to treat chronic diarrhea, morning sickness, and general digestive weakness. Roast it, steam it, or add it to congee for a naturally sweet, Spleen-friendly dish.

🍠 Chinese Yam (山药, Shān Yào) — The Dual Tonic

Chinese yam (huai shan) is one of the most revered foods in TCM because it simultaneously tonifies the Spleen, Lung, and Kidney — making it a rare triple-action tonic. For Late Summer, its primary benefit is strengthening the Spleen and stopping diarrhea. It is neither too warming nor too cooling, making it safe for daily consumption. Slice it into soups, stir-fry it with other vegetables, or powder it and mix into porridge.

🫘 Job's Tears / Coix Seed (薏苡仁, Yì Yǐ Rén) — The Dampness Drainer

Job's tears (yì yǐ rén) is the single most important TCM food for resolving Dampness. It is sweet, slightly cold, and drains Dampness through the urinary system while simultaneously strengthening the Spleen. For Late Summer, it is invaluable — especially for those with Damp-Heat patterns (thick yellow tongue coating, sticky stools, skin eruptions). Cook it into congee, brew it as tea, or use it in soups. Note: Job's tears is slightly cooling, so pair it with warming foods like ginger or cinnamon if your constitution tends toward cold.

🫚 Ginger (生姜, Shēng Jiāng) — The Warming Catalyst

Ginger is the Spleen's best ally. Its warm, acrid nature stimulates digestive fire, stops nausea, and counteracts the chilling effects of cold drinks and raw foods that are so tempting in summer. In TCM, ginger is considered essential for protecting the Spleen from Dampness — it is added to nearly every Spleen-tonifying formula and recipe. For Late Summer, drink fresh ginger tea in the morning, add sliced ginger to congee, and use it liberally in cooking.

⚠️ Foods to Avoid in Late Summer

  • Cold, raw foods: Ice-cold smoothies, salads, and refrigerated drinks directly damage Spleen Yang — the digestive fire needed to transform food. In Late Summer, the Spleen is already burdened by external humidity; adding cold is like pouring water on a struggling flame.
  • Excessive dairy: Milk, cheese, and ice cream are classified as Damp-producing in TCM. They create phlegm, sticky stools, and nasal congestion — all signs of worsening Dampness.
  • Refined sugar and white flour: These are "empty sweets" that provide no Qi while further weakening the Spleen. Unlike whole grains and root vegetables, they generate Dampness without nourishment.
  • Greasy, fried foods: Deep-fried and overly oily foods overwhelm the Spleen's transforming capacity and generate internal Damp-Heat.
  • Excessive fruit juice: Even natural fruit juice is too concentrated in sugar and too cold for the Late Summer Spleen. Eat whole fruits in moderation instead, and avoid refrigerated varieties.

Essential Acupoints for Late Summer Wellness

Acupressure is a powerful complement to dietary therapy for strengthening the Spleen and resolving Dampness. The following four acupoints form the core Late Summer protocol — each addresses a different aspect of Earth element imbalance and can be safely stimulated at home.

1. Sanyinjiao 三阴交 (SP6) — Three Yin Intersection

Location: On the inner lower leg, approximately four finger-widths above the tip of the medial inner ankle bone, just behind the tibia (shinbone).

SP6 is the meeting point of the three Yin meridians of the leg — the Spleen, Liver, and Kidney. This makes it one of the most versatile acupoints in the entire body. For Late Summer, SP6 is prized for its ability to strengthen the Spleen, resolve Dampness, and regulate the digestive system simultaneously. It is also a key point for gynecological health and insomnia. Apply firm, circular pressure with your thumb for 2–3 minutes on each leg, preferably after meals or before bed.

Best for: Bloating, loose stools, fatigue, insomnia, menstrual irregularities, and general Spleen Qi deficiency. Note: SP6 is contraindicated during pregnancy — it stimulates the uterus.

2. Zusanli 足三里 (ST36) — Leg Three Miles

Location: On the outer lower leg, one finger-width lateral to the tibial crest, approximately four finger-widths below the lower border of the kneecap.

ST36 is arguably the most famous acupoint in TCM — and for good reason. It is the master point for the Stomach meridian and is renowned for its broad-spectrum health benefits. For Late Summer, ST36 powerfully strengthens the Spleen and Stomach, tonifies Qi and Blood, boosts immunity, and aids digestion. The point's name — "Leg Three Miles" — reflects the Chinese saying that moxibustion at ST36 gives you the energy to walk three more miles. Stimulate ST36 by pressing firmly with your knuckle or thumb for 3–5 minutes, or use moxibustion for a deeply warming effect.

Best for: Weak digestion, fatigue, poor appetite, bloating, and general vitality. Combine with SP6 for comprehensive Earth element support.

3. Zhongwan 中脘 (CV12) — Middle Cavity

Location: On the midline of the upper abdomen, midway between the navel and the tip of the xiphoid process (the bottom of the sternum), approximately four finger-widths above the navel.

CV12 is the Front-Mu (Alarm) point of the Stomach — the place where Stomach Qi gathers and concentrates. It is the premier acupoint for all digestive issues: bloating, indigestion, acid reflux, nausea, and poor appetite. For Late Summer, massaging CV12 after meals helps the Spleen transform food and prevents Dampness from accumulating in the Middle Jiao. Use gentle clockwise circular motions with your palm — this follows the natural direction of intestinal peristalsis in TCM abdominal massage.

Best for: Post-meal bloating, indigestion, stomach pain that improves with warmth, and acid reflux. Avoid deep pressure on CV12 if you have an active stomach ulcer or acute abdominal pain.

4. Yinlingquan 阴陵泉 (SP9) — Yin Mound Spring

Location: On the inner lower leg, in the depression just below the medial condyle of the tibia (the inner knee bone), behind the shinbone.

SP9 is the most important acupoint for draining Dampness from the body. It is the He-Sea point of the Spleen meridian and is specifically indicated for conditions involving Dampness: edema, urinary difficulty, diarrhea, and the sensation of heaviness. For Late Summer, when external humidity is high and internal Dampness is accumulating, SP9 acts like a drain plug — it opens the water passages and allows Dampness to be eliminated through the bladder. Apply firm pressure for 2–3 minutes on each leg.

Best for: Edema, heavy limbs, urinary difficulty, diarrhea, leukorrhea, and any condition worsened by humid weather. This point is especially effective for the "heavy, sticky" feeling that defines Late Summer Dampness.

Lifestyle Practices for Late Summer Harmony

Food and acupoints are essential, but TCM always emphasizes lifestyle as the foundation of health. The following practices will help you align with the Earth element's natural rhythm and keep your Spleen strong throughout Late Summer and beyond.

1. Eat Warm, Cooked Meals — No More Summer Salads

The single most impactful change you can make for Late Summer is to stop eating cold, raw foods. Your Spleen is like a stove — it needs heat to cook the food. Raw vegetables, cold smoothies, and refrigerated drinks throw cold water on the stove, leaving food half-digested and turning it into Dampness. Switch to warm, cooked meals: soups, stews, congee, steamed vegetables, and warm herbal teas. Even in the heat of August, a warm breakfast of millet porridge will leave you feeling lighter than a cold salad.

2. Eat at Regular Times — Honor the Earth's Rhythm

The Earth element thrives on regularity. The Spleen and Stomach operate on a predictable schedule — in TCM, the Stomach's peak hours are 7–9 AM (when breakfast should be largest) and the Spleen's are 9–11 AM (when transformation is strongest). Eating at erratic times, skipping meals, or late-night snacking disrupts this rhythm and damages Spleen Qi. Aim for three meals at consistent times, with the largest meal in the morning and the lightest at night.

3. Manage Worry — The Emotion of Earth

In TCM, each organ is paired with an emotion — and the Spleen's is worry and overthinking. Chronic worry, excessive mental work, and obsessive rumination directly weaken the Spleen and disrupt digestion. This creates a vicious cycle: Spleen deficiency leads to poor concentration, which causes anxiety and overthinking, which further damages the Spleen. To break the cycle, practice grounding activities in Late Summer: gentle walking in nature, cooking nourishing meals, spending time with loved ones, and setting aside designated "worry time" rather than ruminating all day.

4. Gentle Afternoon Movement — Move the Qi, Don't Sweat It Out

The Earth element benefits from gentle, grounding movement that stimulates the Spleen and Stomach meridians without causing excessive sweating. Vigorous cardio and heavy sweating in Late Summer deplete Qi and Body Fluids — the opposite of what the Earth element needs. Instead, practice gentle walking after meals (this aids digestion in TCM), restorative yoga, or Tai Chi. The Spleen meridian runs along the inner leg — walking gently activates it and promotes healthy digestion.

"The Earth is the mother of all things. It receives the seed, nourishes it without judgment, and brings forth life. To live in harmony with the Earth element is to learn the art of patient, steady nourishment — of the body, the mind, and the spirit."

— Traditional Chinese Medicine principle on the Earth element (土, Tǔ)

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How long does Late Summer last in TCM?

In the traditional Chinese calendar, Late Summer (长夏) is typically assigned to the last 18 days of each of the four seasons — but in common TCM practice, it is most often associated with the humid weeks from late July through mid-September. This is the period when the weather is hottest and most humid, and when the Spleen is most vulnerable to Dampness invasion. Some TCM traditions consider every transitional period between seasons (approximately 18 days) to be a mini-Late Summer governed by the Earth element.

Q: Can I drink iced coffee in Late Summer?

Iced coffee is one of the worst beverages for the Spleen in Late Summer — and yet it is among the most commonly consumed. Coffee is already bitter and draining to the Spleen; adding ice further suppresses digestive fire. If you must have coffee, drink it warm or at room temperature, in the morning, and limit it to one cup. Better alternatives include ginger tea, Job's tears tea, warm water with a slice of fresh ginger, or mild roasted grain teas (like barley tea) that support the Spleen without the diuretic effect of coffee.

Q: Is Dampness the same as edema or water retention?

They are related but not identical. Edema (subcutaneous fluid accumulation) is one manifestation of Dampness, but Dampness in TCM is a broader concept that includes any heavy, sticky, lingering pathogenic factor — including phlegm, greasy tongue coatings, sticky stools, brain fog, and the sensation of heaviness. You can have significant Dampness without visible edema. Conversely, edema can be caused by other TCM patterns (Kidney Yang deficiency, for example) that are not strictly "Dampness." A TCM practitioner can distinguish between these patterns through tongue and pulse diagnosis.

Q: I have acid reflux — is that Dampness or something else?

Acid reflux in TCM is typically classified as Stomach Fire or Stomach Qi Rebelling Upward — the normal downward flow of Stomach Qi is reversed, sending acid into the esophagus. While Dampness can coexist with this pattern (especially if you also have a greasy tongue coating), acid reflux alone is not typically a pure Dampness condition. It often requires a different acupoint strategy — focusing on points that descend Stomach Qi, such as CV12 (Zhongwan) and PC6 (Neiguen). If your acid reflux is accompanied by burning pain, irritability, and a red tongue with yellow coating, it is more likely a Heat pattern than a Dampness pattern.

Q: Can I do moxibustion for Late Summer Spleen deficiency?

Absolutely — moxibustion is one of the most effective therapies for Spleen Yang deficiency and Dampness in Late Summer. The warming, penetrating heat of moxa directly strengthens the Spleen's transforming function and evaporates Dampness the way sunlight evaporates morning dew. The best points for Late Summer moxibustion are ST36 (Zusanli), CV12 (Zhongwan), and SP6 (Sanyinjiao). Apply gentle moxa stick moxibustion to each point for 5–10 minutes, 2–3 times per week. If you are unfamiliar with moxibustion, start with ST36 — it is the safest and most universally beneficial point for this purpose.

Q: What's the difference between Summer Dampness and Late Summer Dampness?

Great question. In TCM, "Summer Dampness" (暑湿) refers to the acute invasion of damp-heat that causes sudden symptoms — nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, fever, and heavy sweating — typically during the hottest weeks of summer. It is an acute condition. "Late Summer Dampness" (湿邪), on the other hand, refers to the chronic, low-grade accumulation of Dampness that develops over weeks of humid weather and poor dietary habits. It manifests as the slow, creeping symptoms we discussed: brain fog, heavy limbs, loose stools, and fatigue. Summer Dampness is like a flood; Late Summer Dampness is like damp soil that never fully dries. The treatment approach differs: acute Summer Dampness requires clearing heat and resolving Dampness quickly, while chronic Late Summer Dampness requires slowly warming and strengthening the Spleen over time.
