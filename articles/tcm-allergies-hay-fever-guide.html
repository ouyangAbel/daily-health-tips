TCM for Allergies & Hay Fever: Natural Relief from Seasonal Allergies

📅 August 2, 2026 • TCM Remedies
Advertisement Space (728x90)

Every year, as the seasons shift, millions of people find themselves reaching for tissues, eye drops, and antihistamines. Seasonal allergies — known medically as allergic rhinitis (过敏性鼻炎) and commonly called hay fever (花粉症) — affect an estimated 10–30% of the world's population. Symptoms like sneezing, itchy eyes, nasal congestion, and postnasal drip can drain your energy, disrupt sleep, and make even the most beautiful spring or autumn day feel miserable.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) offers a fundamentally different approach. Rather than merely suppressing histamine release, TCM views allergies as a sign of Wei Qi (卫气) deficiency — a weakness in the body's protective energy that allows external pathogens to invade. By strengthening your Wei Qi, clearing pathogenic factors, and balancing the organs involved, TCM aims to address the root cause of allergies, not just the symptoms. The result? Long-lasting relief that improves with each passing season.

This comprehensive guide will walk you through how TCM understands allergies, the most common patterns of imbalance, and a complete toolkit of acupressure points, herbal teas, dietary strategies, and lifestyle practices to help you breathe freely and enjoy every season.

Key Takeaways

  • In TCM, allergies are primarily caused by Wei Qi (卫气) deficiency combined with invasion of external pathogens — mainly Wind-Cold (风寒) or Wind-Hat (风热)
  • The Lung (肺) governs the nose and opens into it; the Spleen (脾) produces Wei Qi; the Kidney (肾) provides the foundation for immune resilience
  • Three main patterns are recognized: Wind-Cold invasion, Wind-Hat invasion, and chronic Qi/Yang deficiency with lingering pathogen
  • Key acupoints like Yingxiang (LI20), Hegu (LI4), Lieque (LU7), and Zusanli (ST36) can relieve congestion and strengthen immunity within minutes
  • Herbal formulas like Yu Ping Feng San (Jade Screen) and Cang Er Zi San (Xanthium Powder) have been used for centuries to prevent and treat allergies
  • Avoiding cold, raw foods and damp-producing dairy while incorporating warming, Qi-tonifying foods is essential for allergy prevention
  • The best time to strengthen Wei Qi is during the "off-season" — treating allergies months before they typically flare

How TCM Understands Allergies: The Battle at the Surface

In Western medicine, allergies are an overreaction of the immune system to harmless substances like pollen, dust mites, or pet dander. The immune system produces IgE antibodies, triggering mast cells to release histamine — hence the sneezing, itching, and congestion. Antihistamines work by blocking this chemical cascade.

TCM sees the same phenomenon through a different lens. The body's surface is protected by Wei Qi (卫气) — literally "defensive energy" — that circulates in the skin and muscles, acting as a barrier against external pathogens. Wei Qi opens and closes the pores, regulates body temperature, and fights off invaders before they can penetrate deeper. When Wei Qi is strong, you resist seasonal changes effortlessly. When it is weak, external pathogens — described as Wind (风), Cold (寒), Heat (热), Damp (湿), or Dryness (燥) — ride into the body and lodge in the nose, throat, and eyes, producing allergy symptoms.

The Lung (肺) is the key organ in this process. The Lung governs Qi and respiration, controls the skin and body hair, and opens into the nose. When Lung Qi is deficient, the nose loses its proper function — fluids accumulate (producing clear runny nose), the sense of smell weakens, and the nasal passages become reactive. The Spleen (脾) is the source of Wei Qi production — if the Spleen is weak from poor diet or overthinking, Wei Qi becomes deficient. And the Kidney (肾) provides the deep constitutional foundation for immune resilience — Kidney Yang deficiency means the body cannot generate enough defensive fire to keep pathogens at bay.

"When Wei Qi is strong, the body is defended. External pathogens cannot enter. When Wei Qi is deficient, Wind rides in through the open gates, and the nose runs, the eyes itch, and the sneezes follow."

— Huangdi Neijing (黄帝内经), the Yellow Emperor's Inner Canon, c. 200 BCE

Three Common Allergy Patterns in TCM

TCM identifies distinct patterns of allergic rhinitis, each with its own set of symptoms, triggers, and treatment strategies. Understanding your pattern is essential for choosing the right self-care approach.

1. Wind-Cold Invasion (风寒袭肺) — The Classic Cold-Type Allergy

Symptoms: Sneezing with profuse clear watery nasal discharge, nasal congestion, no thirst, no sore throat, possibly mild fever and chills, headache, pale tongue with thin white coating, floating tight pulse. Symptoms worsen in cold weather or air conditioning.

Treatment principle: Release the exterior, dispel Wind-Cold, and restore Lung Qi diffusion. The classic formula is Ma Huang Tang (Ephedra Decoction) for acute cases or Gui Zhi Tang (Cinnamon Twig Decoction) for chronic cases with underlying deficiency.

Key acupoints: LU7 (Lieque), LI4 (Hegu), BL12 (Fengmen) — these points release the exterior and open the nasal passages.

2. Wind-Hat Invasion (风热犯肺) — The Summer Allergy Pattern

Symptoms: Nasal congestion with thick yellow discharge, sore throat, slight thirst, itchy red eyes, sneezing, red tongue with thin yellow coating, floating rapid pulse. More common in summer or when exposed to heat and pollution.

Treatment principle: Dispel Wind-Heat, clear the head and eyes, and restore Lung diffusion. The classic formula is Yin Qiao San (Honeysuckle and Forsythia Powder) for mild cases or Cang Er Zi San (Xanthium Powder) for chronic nasal congestion with thick discharge.

Key acupoints: LI4 (Hegu), LU7 (Lieque), GV23 (Shangxing) — these clear Heat from the head and open the nose.

3. Qi Deficiency with Lingering Pathogen (肺气虚寒) — The Chronic Weakness Pattern

Symptoms: Chronic sneezing and runny nose (often worse in the morning), extreme sensitivity to temperature changes, fatigue, shortness of breath, spontaneous sweating, low voice, pale tongue with white coating, weak pulse. This pattern describes the person who "catches every cold" and whose allergies never quite go away.

Treatment principle: Tonify Lung Qi, strengthen Wei Qi, and support the Spleen and Kidney. The classic preventive formula is Yu Ping Feng San (Jade Screen Powder) — a simple three-herb formula that builds the immune shield. Combined with Cang Er Zi San for the nasal symptoms, this is the gold standard for chronic allergic rhinitis.

Key acupoints: ST36 (Zusanli), LU9 (Taiyuan), BL13 (Feishu), GV14 (Dazhui) — these tonify Qi and strengthen the body's defenses.

Essential Acupoints for Allergy Relief: Your At-Home Toolkit

Acupressure is one of the fastest ways to relieve allergy symptoms — often providing noticeable relief within 5–10 minutes of stimulation. These points work by restoring the proper flow of Qi through the Lung meridian, opening the nasal passages, and strengthening the body's defensive energy.

1. Yingxiang 迎香 (LI20) — Welcome the Fragrance

Location: On either side of the nostril, in the nasolabial groove (the "smile line") at the widest part of the nostril.

The single most important acupoint for nasal congestion and rhinitis in all of TCM. LI20 is the meeting point of the Large Intestine and Stomach meridians, located directly beside the nasal passages. It opens the nose, dispels Wind, and restores the sense of smell. Press firmly with both index fingers, using small circular motions, for 2–3 minutes. You should feel immediate relief as the nasal passages open. This is the first point to press whenever congestion strikes.

Best for: Nasal congestion, loss of smell, runny nose, sneezing, and sinus pressure. Press every 2–3 hours during allergy season for continuous relief.

2. Hegu 合谷 (LI4) — The Union Valley

Location: On the back of the hand, between the thumb and index finger, in the fleshy web at the highest point of the muscle when the thumb and index finger are brought together.

LI4 is the Source (Yuan) point of the Large Intestine meridian and one of the most powerful acupoints in the entire body. It is called the "command point of the face and head" — meaning it treats virtually every condition of the face, nose, eyes, and mouth. It releases the exterior, dispels Wind-Cold and Wind-Heat, and relieves pain. For allergies, it is almost always combined with LI20 for maximum nasal opening effect.

Best for: Headache, sinus pain, nasal congestion, sneezing, and facial pressure. Press firmly for 2–3 minutes on each hand. Contraindicated in pregnancy.

3. Lieque 列缺 (LU7) — The Broken Sequence

Location: On the inner forearm, approximately 1.5 finger-widths above the wrist crease, in the depression on the thumb side of the radius bone (just above the styloid process).

LU7 is the Luo-Connecting point of the Lung meridian and one of the master points of the Conception Vessel (Ren Mai). It is the premier point for restoring Lung Qi diffusion — the Lung's ability to send fluids downward and open the nasal passages. It is specifically indicated for sneezing, runny nose, cough, and sore throat. Combined with LI4 (Hegu), it forms the famous "Four Gates" combination for releasing the exterior.

Best for: Sneezing, clear runny nose, sore throat, cough, and any symptom involving Lung Qi failing to descend. Press gently for 2–3 minutes on each arm.

4. Zusanli 足三里 (ST36) — The Leg Three Miles

Location: On the outer side of the lower leg, approximately 3 finger-widths below the kneecap, one finger-width lateral to the shinbone (tibia).

ST36 is one of the most important acupoints for overall vitality and immune function. It tonifies Qi and Blood, strengthens the Spleen and Stomach, and builds Wei Qi (defensive energy). For chronic allergies rooted in Qi deficiency, ST36 is essential — it strengthens the body's ability to resist external pathogens in the first place. Many practitioners moxa ST36 daily during allergy season as a preventive measure.

Best for: Fatigue, weak immunity, poor digestion, susceptibility to colds and allergies, and general weakness. Press firmly for 3–5 minutes on each leg, or apply moxibustion for 5–10 minutes daily.

Herbal Teas & Formulas: Nature's Antihistamines

TCM has a rich tradition of herbal formulas specifically designed for allergies and nasal congestion. While complex formulas should be prescribed by a qualified TCM practitioner, several safe and effective herbal teas can be used at home for daily relief and prevention.

Yu Ping Feng San Tea (玉屏风散) — The Jade Screen Formula

Composition: Astragalus root (黄芪, Huang Qi) 12g, White Atractylodes (白术, Bai Zhu) 12g, Ledebouriella root (防风, Fang Feng) 6g.

The classic preventive formula for allergies. Yu Ping Feng San translates to "Jade Screen Powder" — it creates a protective screen around the body like a jade shield. Astragalus tonifies Lung Qi and Wei Qi; White Atractylodes strengthens the Spleen to produce more Wei Qi; Ledebouriella expels lingering Wind pathogens. This formula is ideal for the weeks and months before allergy season begins — it builds resilience so that when pollen counts rise, your body can handle them. Drink one cup daily for 4–6 weeks before your typical allergy season.

Xanthium & Magnolia Tea (苍耳子辛夷茶) — The Nose-Opening Brew

Composition: Xanthium fruit (苍耳子, Cang Er Zi) 6g, Magnolia flower (辛夷花, Xin Yi Hua) 6g, Angelica dahurica (白芷, Bai Zhi) 6g, Mint (薄荷, Bo He) 3g.

This is a simplified version of Cang Er Zi San, one of the most famous formulas for chronic nasal congestion and thick nasal discharge. Xanthium and Magnolia flower are the premier herbs for opening the nasal passages and expelling Wind-Damp. Angelica dahurica clears the sinuses and relieves headache. Mint provides a cooling, opening sensation. Steep in hot water for 10 minutes and inhale the steam while drinking. Use during acute allergy flare-ups.

Chrysanthemum & Cassia Seed Tea (菊花决明茶) — For Itchy Eyes

Composition: Chrysanthemum flowers (菊花, Ju Hua) 5g, Cassia seeds (决明子, Jue Ming Zi) 5g, Green tea 3g.

For allergy sufferers whose main symptom is itchy, red, watery eyes, this tea is a blessing. Chrysanthemum clears Wind-Heat from the eyes and relieves redness and itching. Cassia seeds clear the eyes and moisten the intestines. Green tea provides a mild astringent action that helps dry up excess tearing. This tea is particularly effective for Wind-Hat pattern allergies with prominent eye symptoms.

Foods That Fight Allergies: The TCM Dietary Approach

In TCM dietary therapy, what you eat directly affects your Wei Qi, your Spleen's ability to produce energy, and your Lung's ability to defend against external pathogens. The right diet can significantly reduce allergy symptoms, while the wrong foods can make them worse.

Allergy-Fighting Foods to Include

  • Ginger (生姜, Sheng Jiang): Warms the Lung, releases the exterior, and dispels Wind-Cold. Perfect for cold-type allergies with clear runny nose. Make ginger tea with brown sugar and red dates.
  • Scallion Whites (葱白, Cong Bai): The white parts of scallions release the exterior and open the nasal passages. Combined with ginger, they form a classic home remedy for the onset of allergy symptoms or cold.
  • Pumpkin & Sweet Potato (南瓜/红薯): Tonify Spleen Qi and strengthen Wei Qi production. Their orange color indicates their connection to the Spleen and Earth element.
  • Walnuts (核桃, He Tao): Tonify Kidney Yang and warm the Lung. Essential for chronic allergies with cold constitution. Eat 5–6 daily during winter and spring.
  • Astragalus (黄芪, Huang Qi): The premier herb for tonifying Wei Qi. Add to soups and stews during allergy season. Remove before serving.
  • Honey with Propolis: Local raw honey may help desensitize the immune system to local pollens. Propolis has natural anti-inflammatory properties.
  • Pineapple: Contains bromelain, which reduces nasal swelling and thins mucus — recognized by both Western science and TCM dietary therapy.

Foods to Avoid During Allergy Season

  • Cold, raw foods: Ice water, ice cream, raw salads, and cold smoothies damage Spleen Yang and weaken Wei Qi. Your digestive system must warm food before it can extract energy — cold foods steal that energy.
  • Dairy products: Milk, cheese, and yogurt produce Dampness and Phlegm in TCM, worsening nasal congestion and postnasal drip. If you're allergic, dairy is your enemy during flare-ups.
  • Sugar and refined carbohydrates: Excess sweet taste weakens the Spleen and feeds Dampness. Avoid candy, pastries, and sugary drinks.
  • Greasy, fried foods: These generate internal Heat and Dampness, creating a toxic environment where allergens thrive.
  • Excessive spicy foods: While moderate ginger and scallion whites help, too much spicy food disperses Lung Qi and can worsen symptoms.

Lifestyle Practices for Allergy Prevention

Beyond acupressure, herbs, and diet, TCM offers several lifestyle practices that strengthen Wei Qi and reduce allergy susceptibility over time.

1. Neti Pot with Warm Salt Water (温盐水洗鼻)

Using a neti pot with warm saline solution to rinse the nasal passages can physically remove pollen, dust, and other allergens. In TCM terms, this clears Dampness and Heat from the nasal passages. Use distilled or previously boiled water with a quarter teaspoon of non-iodized salt. Rinse once daily during allergy season for prevention and twice daily during acute symptoms.

2. Moxibustion on GV14 and ST36 (艾灸大椎足三里)

Moxibustion — the burning of mugwort (艾叶) near acupoints — is one of the most powerful ways to strengthen Wei Qi and prevent allergies. GV14 (Dazhui) on the upper back is the meeting point of all Yang meridian — moxaing it warms the entire body's Yang and boosts immunity. ST36 (Zusanli) tonifies Qi and Blood. Apply moxa to both points for 5–10 minutes daily during the months before your allergy season begins. Many patients report that after one season of preventive moxibustion, their allergies are significantly reduced or eliminated the following year.

3. Avoid Wind & Weather Extremes (避风)

TCM emphasizes "avoiding Wind" (避风) as a fundamental health principle. In the Huangdi Neijing, it says: "When the body is empty, the evil Wind seizes the opportunity and enters." Protect your neck and upper back from cold wind by wearing scarves in windy weather, avoiding direct drafts from air conditioning, and covering your head on cold days. The area between the neck and upper back — where the Wind-related acupoints (Fengmen, Fengchi) are located — is especially vulnerable.

4. Practice Qigong or Tai Chi for Wei Qi (练功固表)

Regular Qigong practice — especially forms that focus on breathing and posture — strengthens Lung Qi and Wei Qi. The Baduanjin (Eight Brocades) and Liu Zi Jue (Six Healing Sounds) are particularly beneficial. Even 15 minutes of daily practice can significantly improve your body's ability to resist external pathogens over time.

⚠️ Important Note

While the herbal formulas and acupoints described in this article are generally safe when used appropriately, chronic or severe allergies should be evaluated by a qualified healthcare provider. If you experience difficulty breathing, wheezing, or swelling of the face or throat, seek emergency medical attention immediately. Herbal formulas like Yu Ping Feng San are best used under the guidance of a licensed TCM practitioner, especially if you are taking other medications or have complex health conditions.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: Can TCM cure my seasonal allergies permanently?

TCM aims to address the root cause of allergies — Wei Qi deficiency and organ imbalance — rather than just suppressing symptoms. Many patients experience significant improvement or complete resolution of symptoms after consistent TCM treatment over 1–2 allergy seasons. The key is to begin treatment during the "off-season" (the time of year when you have no symptoms) and continue preventive practices through the next season. Acupuncture, herbal medicine, dietary changes, and moxibustion work best as a combined approach. While some patients achieve long-term remission, others may need ongoing seasonal maintenance.

Q: How soon can I expect relief from acupressure?

Acupressure on LI20 (Yingxiang) and LI4 (Hegu) can provide noticeable relief from nasal congestion within 5–10 minutes of stimulation. For best results, press firmly (to a tolerable level) for 2–3 minutes per point, multiple times throughout the day. However, chronic allergy patterns require consistent daily practice for 3–4 weeks before lasting improvement is seen. Acupressure works best as both an acute remedy (for immediate symptom relief) and a long-term preventive practice.

Q: Is Yu Ping Feng San safe to take daily?

Yu Ping Feng San (Jade Screen Powder) is considered one of the safest and most gentle formulas in TCM. Its three ingredients — Astragalus, White Atractylodes, and Ledebouriella — are all food-grade herbs with a long history of safe use. It is commonly taken daily for 1–3 months as a preventive measure. However, if you have an acute infection with fever, stop taking it (it is a tonic and may "lock in" the pathogen). Also consult a practitioner if you have autoimmune conditions or are taking immunosuppressive medications.

Q: What if I'm allergic to the herbs used in TCM?

While rare, herb allergies do occur. If you experience any new symptoms (rash, itching, digestive upset) after taking an herbal formula, stop immediately and consult a qualified TCM practitioner. They can modify the formula to remove the problematic herb while maintaining the therapeutic effect. Single-herb teas (like ginger tea or chrysanthemum tea) are generally safer than complex formulas.

Q: Can children use these TCM allergy remedies?

Yes, TCM is widely used for pediatric allergies in China and around the world. However, children require significantly lower doses of herbs and gentler acupressure. For children under 12, acupressure is preferred over herbal formulas — gentle massage on LI20, ST36, and LU7 is safe and effective. Dietary therapy (ginger scallion tea, pumpkin soup) is also appropriate. Consult a pediatric TCM practitioner for herbal formula prescriptions for children.

Q: How long does it take to see improvement in allergy symptoms?

With consistent practice — daily acupressure, herbal tea, dietary adjustments, and moxibustion — most patients notice significant improvement within 3–4 weeks. The first symptom to improve is usually nasal congestion, followed by reduced sneezing and less itchy eyes. For complete resolution of chronic allergy patterns, 2–3 months of consistent treatment is typical. Starting treatment 4–6 weeks before your typical allergy season begins gives the best results.
