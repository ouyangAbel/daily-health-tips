Autumn Wellness in TCM: Nourish the Lung, Conquer Dryness & Prepare for Winter

📅 July 28, 2026 • Seasonal Wellness
Advertisement Space (728x90)

As the heat of summer fades and crisp morning air signals the arrival of autumn, Traditional Chinese Medicine offers a profound framework for understanding how to adapt our bodies to the changing season. In TCM, autumn belongs to the Metal element (金, Jīn) and is governed by the Lung (肺, Fèi) and Large Intestine (大肠, Dà Cháng). The season's dominant pathogen is Dryness (燥, Zào) — a subtle but powerful force that, left unchecked, can drain the body's fluids, weaken the immune system, and set the stage for winter illness.

Autumn wellness is not merely about treating symptoms as they arise; it is about proactive seasonal nourishment — what the ancients called "nourishing the Yang in spring and summer, and nourishing the Yin in autumn and winter" (春夏养阳, 秋冬养阴). By understanding TCM's seasonal framework, you can harness the unique energy of autumn to strengthen your Lung Qi, preserve bodily fluids, fortify your immune defenses, and build a reservoir of vitality that carries you through the cold months ahead.

This guide draws on centuries of TCM wisdom to provide you with practical, actionable strategies for autumn health — from dietary therapy and healing foods to acupressure, lifestyle adjustments, and the emotional dimensions of Metal element wellness. Whether you are prone to seasonal allergies, dry skin, autumn coughs, or simply want to optimize your health for the changing season, these ancient practices offer timeless guidance.

Key Takeaways

  • Autumn corresponds to the Metal element, the Lung, and the Large Intestine in TCM — Dryness (燥) is the seasonal pathogen
  • The Lung governs Qi, respiration, the skin, and the body's defensive Wei Qi — making Lung nourishment the cornerstone of autumn immunity
  • Two types of autumn dryness exist: Warm Dryness (温燥) in early autumn and Cool Dryness (凉燥) in late autumn — each requires a different therapeutic approach
  • Seven evidence-informed autumn wellness benefits range from respiratory health and skin vitality to immune strengthening and emotional balance
  • Moistening, Yin-nourishing foods — pears, honey, lily bulbs, white fungus, sesame, and honey — are TCM's primary dietary weapons against seasonal dryness
  • Key acupoints like LU9 (Taiyuan), LU7 (Lieque), and LI4 (Hegou) support Lung function and can be stimulated at home
  • The emotion of grief is associated with the Lung in TCM — autumn is the season for emotional release and letting go

The TCM Theory of Autumn: Metal, the Lung, and the Dryness Pathogen

In the Five Elements (五行, Wǔ Xíng) framework, autumn belongs to the Metal element — a phase of contraction, refinement, and gathering. Just as trees draw their sap inward and leaves fall to conserve energy, the human body in autumn begins to shift from the expansive, outward energy of summer to a more inward, consolidating state. The Huangdi Neijing describes autumn as the season of "recollection and harvest" (收敛, Shōu Liǎn), a time to gather and store.

The Lung is the Yin organ of the Metal element and holds the office of "Minister and Chancellor" (相傅之相) in the body's physiological government. Its functions in TCM extend far beyond simple respiration:

  • Governs Qi and Respiration (主气, 司呼吸): The Lung draws in clear Qi from the air and governs the body's entire Qi metabolism. Weak Lung Qi manifests as fatigue, shortness of breath, and a weak voice.
  • Controls the Skin and Body Hair (主皮毛): The Lung disperses Wei Qi (defensive energy) to the skin surface, regulating sweating and protecting against external pathogens. Skin health and immunity are directly linked to Lung function.
  • Opens into the Nose (开窍于鼻): The nose is the gateway to the Lung. Autumn nasal dryness, sneezing, and congestion all reflect the Lung's vulnerability during this season.
  • Regulates the Water Passages (通调水道): The Lung acts as an upper source of water, distributing fluids downward to the Kidney and Bladder. When this function is impaired, fluid retention or dryness can result.

Dryness (燥, Zào) is the autumn pathogen and the Lung's greatest enemy. Among the six pernicious influences (六淫, Liù Yín) — Wind, Cold, Summer Heat, Dampness, Dryness, and Fire — Dryness is uniquely associated with autumn. It consumes body fluids (津液, Jīn Yè), leading to dryness of the skin, throat, lips, stool, and respiratory passages. Because the Lung is a "delicate organ" (娇脏, Jiāo Zàng) that prefers moisture and dislikes dryness, it is the first organ affected.

"In autumn, the Qi of the Lung is dominant. Nourish the Lung, collect the Yin, calm the Qi, and gather the spirit. This is the way of living in harmony with the autumn season."

— Adapted from the Huangdi Neijing (黄帝内经), Su Wen, Chapter 2

7 Major Health Benefits of Autumn Wellness Practices

By aligning your lifestyle, diet, and self-care with TCM's autumn principles, you address the seasonal vulnerabilities of the Metal element while building resilience for the entire year. Below are seven key benefits grounded in both classical TCM theory and modern physiological understanding.

1. Strengthened Respiratory Immunity

The Lung governs Wei Qi — the body's surface-level defensive energy that resists external pathogens. In autumn, when cold air and dryness assault the respiratory passages, a well-nourished Lung produces robust Wei Qi that forms a protective barrier against colds, flu, and seasonal allergies. Studies on TCM autumn dietary therapy have found that regular consumption of moistening, Lung-nourishing foods correlates with reduced upper respiratory infection frequency and severity.

2. Relief from Seasonal Dryness

Autumn dryness manifests as dry skin, chapped lips, dry throat, dry cough, constipation, and brittle hair. TCM's moistening (润, Rùn) foods and practices directly address these symptoms by nourishing Yin fluids and supporting the Lung's fluid-distribution function. Unlike topical moisturizers that address surface dryness, TCM approaches resolve the underlying fluid deficiency at its root.

3. Enhanced Skin Vitality

Because the Lung "controls the skin and body hair" (主皮毛), Lung health is directly reflected in the skin's appearance and function. Autumn Lung nourishment clears dryness, reduces flakiness, and promotes a supple, moisturized complexion from within. The TCM principle of "the Lung manifests in the skin" means that autumn skincare begins with Lung nourishment, not cosmetic products.

4. Improved Digestive Function

The Large Intestine, the Yang partner of the Lung in the Metal element, governs the transformation and elimination of waste. Autumn dryness frequently causes constipation as fluids are diverted from the stool to compensate for overall dryness. Nourishing Lung Yin and consuming moistening foods naturally lubricates the intestines and restores regular bowel function — a gentle, food-based alternative to harsh laxatives.

5. Emotional Release and Mental Clarity

The Metal element governs the emotion of grief and sadness (悲, Bēi). Autumn is the season when unprocessed grief tends to surface — and also the season when the Lung has the greatest capacity for emotional release. By nourishing the Lung and practicing autumn wellness, you create the energetic space to let go of what no longer serves you, just as trees release their leaves. This emotional clearing brings mental clarity, acceptance, and a deep sense of inner spaciousness.

6. Preparation for Winter Health

TCM sees the seasons as an interconnected continuum — autumn nourishment directly determines winter resilience. The Kidney, which governs winter, receives the essence (Jing) and fluids preserved during autumn through the Lung's descending function. By nourishing Lung Yin in autumn, you send nourishment downward to the Kidney, ensuring a warm, energetic winter free from chronic cough, asthma flare-ups, and deep fatigue. As the saying goes: "Nourish the autumn to secure the winter."

7. Sinus and Nasal Health

Since the Lung "opens into the nose," autumn Lung nourishment directly benefits nasal passages. Many people suffer from autumn sinus congestion, nosebleeds, or allergic rhinitis as dryness irritates the nasal mucosa. TCM autumn foods and acupoints that moisten the Lung also moisten the nasal passages, reducing the frequency and severity of these seasonal complaints.

Understanding Warm Dryness vs. Cool Dryness: Two Autumn Patterns

Not all autumn dryness is the same. TCM distinguishes between two distinct patterns, and recognizing which one you are experiencing is essential for choosing the right foods and remedies.

🌤️ Warm Dryness (温燥) — Early Autumn

Warm Dryness occurs in early autumn when residual summer heat lingers in the air. Symptoms include: dry cough with sticky yellow phlegm, dry throat with thirst, dry nose, mild fever, slight headache, dry skin that feels warm, irritability, and dark yellow urine. The treatment principle is to clear heat and moisten dryness using cool, sweet, moistening foods like pears, honey, lily bulbs, and white fungus. Avoid spicy, fried, and overly warming foods.

❄️ Cool Dryness (凉燥) — Late Autumn

Cool Dryness arrives in late autumn as temperatures drop and cold air combines with dryness. Symptoms include: dry cough with clear watery phlegm, dry nose and lips without thirst, chills without sweating, headache, and dry skin that feels cool to the touch. The treatment principle is to warm the Lung and transform phlegm while gently moistening. Use warming-moistening foods like almonds, Sichuan fritillary bulb (川贝), tangerine peel (陈皮), ginger, and walnuts. Avoid cold, raw foods.

Autumn Healing Foods: TCM's Moistening Apothecary

TCM dietary therapy offers an extraordinary pharmacopeia of autumn foods that specifically target the Lung and counteract dryness. These are not ordinary ingredients — each has been used for centuries to nourish Yin fluids, moisten the Metal element, and fortify Wei Qi. Below are the most important autumn foods to incorporate into your daily meals.

🍐 Asian Pear (雪梨 Xuě Lí)

Properties: Sweet, slightly cold. Enters the Lung and Stomach meridians.

The quintessential autumn fruit in TCM. Asian pears are nature's Lung moistener — their juicy, cooling flesh directly nourishes Lung Yin and clears autumn dryness. The classic remedy "Pear and Fritillary Bullybulb Steamed Pear" (川贝炖雪梨) is a beloved household treatment for autumn cough and dry throat. For general prevention, simply eating one pear daily during autumn provides ongoing moistening support. Pears also contain sorbitol and fiber that promote healthy bowel movements.

🍯 Honey (蜂蜜 Fēng Mì)

Properties: Sweet, neutral. Enters the Lung, Spleen, and Large Intestine meridians.

Honey is TCM's premier moistening and harmonizing substance. It nourishes the Lung, lubricates the Large Intestine (relieving constipation), tonifies the Spleen, and detoxifies. In autumn, a teaspoon of raw honey dissolved in warm water — never boiling, which destroys its enzymes — each morning provides gentle, sustained moistening of the respiratory and digestive tracts. Honey also enhances the effects of other herbs it is combined with, which is why it is frequently used as a medium for herbal pills.

🌸 Lily Bulb (百合 Bǎi Hé)

Properties: Sweet, slightly cold. Enters the Lung and Heart meridians.

Fresh or dried lily bulb is one of the most cherished Lung-nourishing foods in TCM. It moistens the Lung, stops cough, clears heat, and calms the spirit (Shen). Lily bulb is especially valuable for autumn dry cough that disturbs sleep, as it addresses both the respiratory dryness and the accompanying restlessness. A classic autumn recipe is Lily Bulb and Lotus Seed Soup (百合莲子羹), simmered with rock sugar — a delicious, therapeutic dessert that nourishes Lung Yin and calms the mind.

🍄 White Fungus / Snow Ear (银耳 Yín Ěr)

Properties: Sweet, neutral. Enters the Lung and Stomach meridians.

Known as "poor man's bird's nest," white fungus is a powerhouse of plant-based collagen and polysaccharides that moisten the Lung, nourish Yin, and beautify the skin. In autumn, white fungus soup simmered with red dates, goji berries, and rock sugar is a traditional remedy for dry cough, dry skin, and thirst. Modern research confirms that white fungus polysaccharides enhance immune function and have anti-inflammatory properties. Simmer for 1–2 hours until the fungus releases its gelatinous essence.

🥜 Sesame (芝麻 Zhī Ma)

Properties: Sweet, neutral. Enters the Liver and Kidney meridians; also moistens the Large Intestine.

Both black sesame (黑芝麻) and white sesame (白芝麻) are prized in TCM autumn dietary therapy. Sesame nourishes the Liver and Kidney, enriches Yin and Blood, lubricates the Large Intestine, and darkens hair. For autumn constipation and dry skin, a teaspoon of toasted sesame seeds or sesame oil daily provides gentle, sustained moistening. Black sesame is particularly valued for its Kidney-tonifying effects, making it ideal for older adults or those with lower back weakness.

🥛 Milk and Dairy (牛乳 Niú Rǔ)

Properties: Sweet, neutral. Enters the Lung and Stomach meridians.

TCM has used milk as a Lung-moistening, Yin-nourishing remedy for over two thousand years. The ancient text Shennong Bencao Jing records that milk "supplements the body, benefits the Lung, and moistens the intestines." Warm milk with a teaspoon of honey before bed is a classic autumn remedy for dry throat, night cough, and insomnia. For those with lactose intolerance, small amounts of aged dairy (like Parmesan or aged goat cheese) are often better tolerated.

"In the third month of autumn, the weather is urgent and the people must rise early and retire late, maintaining a calm and composed spirit to mitigate the harshness of autumn."

— Huangdi Neijing (黄帝内经), Su Wen, Chapter 2

Essential Autumn Acupoints: Your At-Home Wellness Map

Acupressure is a gentle, effective way to support your Lung during autumn. The following points are safe for home use and directly address autumn dryness, cough, and immune weakness. Apply steady, moderate pressure with your fingertip for 2–3 minutes per point, breathing deeply throughout.

1. Taiyuan 太渊 (LU9) — Supreme Abyss

Location: On the wrist crease, in the radial artery depression on the thumb side (the "pulse point" of the radial artery at the wrist).

LU9 is the Yuan-Source (原穴) point of the Lung meridian and the master point for Lung Qi. It is the single most important acupoint for nourishing Lung Yin and strengthening respiratory function. Stimulating LU9 moistens dry throat, stops dry cough, and tonifies Lung Qi for improved breathing. It is also one of the Eight Confluence Points, meeting with the Vessel of Pulse (脉会太渊), making it invaluable for overall vitality and circulation.

Best for: Dry cough, weak voice, shortness of breath, wheezing, low energy, and weak pulse. Apply gentle pressure — do not press hard over the artery.

2. Lieque 列缺 (LU7) — Broken Sequence

Location: On the radial side of the forearm, approximately 1.5 cun above the wrist crease, in the depression between the tendons of the brachioradialis and abductor pollicis longus (found by crossing the index fingers and thumbs of both hands — the point is at the tip of the index finger on the opposite arm).

LU7 is the Luo-Connecting (络穴) point of the Lung meridian and one of the most clinically versatile points in all of TCM. It opens the Conception Vessel (Ren Mai), descends Lung Qi to stop cough and wheezing, and disperses Wind from the exterior — making it ideal for autumn colds, headaches, and neck stiffness. As a Confluence Point meeting with the Conception Vessel, LU7 is also the "Master Point" of the Four Command Points for the head and neck.

Best for: Autumn headache, stiff neck, sinus congestion, dry cough, and the initial stages of a cold. Combine with LI4 for a powerful immune-boosting pair.

3. Hegou 合谷 (LI4) — Union Valley

Location: On the back of the hand, between the first and second metacarpal bones, in the middle of the second metacarpal bone on the radial side (approximately at the highest point of the muscle when the thumb and index finger are brought together).

LI4 is the Yuan-Source point of the Large Intestine meridian and one of the most famous acupoints in TCM. It powerfully moves Qi and Blood, releases the exterior (dispels pathogens), regulates the face and head, and relieves pain. In autumn, LI4 is used to strengthen Wei Qi, prevent colds, and relieve the headache and sinus congestion that accompany autumn dryness. The classical pairing of LI4 (Large Intestine) with LU7 (Lung) — their paired meridians — creates a comprehensive Metal element treatment.

Best for: Autumn colds, headaches, sinus pain, facial dryness, and general immune strengthening. Contraindicated in pregnancy.

4. Zusanli 足三里 (ST36) — Leg Three Miles

Location: On the outer lower leg, one finger-width lateral to the tibial crest, approximately four finger-widths below the lower border of the kneecap.

While ST36 belongs to the Earth element rather than Metal, its role in autumn wellness cannot be overstated. As the master point of the Stomach meridian, ST36 strengthens digestion, tonifies Qi and Blood, and fortifies Wei Qi — the defensive energy that the Lung disperses to the skin. In autumn, nourishing the Spleen-Earth "Mother" supports the Lung-Metal "son" through the Five Element mother-son relationship. Regular acupressure or moxibustion at ST36 in autumn builds a broad-spectrum immune foundation that prevents winter illness.

Best for: General immune strengthening, digestive support, fatigue, and building resilience against seasonal illness. Pair with LU9 for comprehensive autumn wellness.

Autumn Lifestyle Practices: Aligning with the Season's Energy

Beyond diet and acupoints, TCM autumn wellness extends to the rhythm of daily life. The Huangdi Neijing provides specific lifestyle guidance for the season:

🌅 Sleep Early and Rise Early (早卧早起)

Autumn days grow shorter, and the body's circadian rhythm should follow. The classic instruction is to "sleep early with the chickens and rise at cockcrow" — approximately 10 PM to 6 AM. Going to bed earlier respects the contracting Yin energy of the season and allows the Lung and Liver to perform their nightly regenerative functions. Rising early aligns with the clear, crisp autumn morning energy and supports Lung Qi circulation.

🧘 Calm the Spirit and Gather Intent (使志安宁)

Autumn is the season of letting go — of releasing excess emotion, unnecessary attachments, and mental clutter. Practice quiet meditation, gentle breathwork, or simply sit in stillness for 10 minutes daily. Avoid overstimulation and excessive socializing, which scatter the inward-moving autumn energy. The TCM ideal is a state of "peaceful calm" (安宁, Ān Níng) that mirrors the stillness of autumn evenings.

🌬️ Protect Against Cold and Dryness (避寒就温)

As temperatures fluctuate in autumn, protect your neck, throat, and lower back from cold wind — the entry point for Wind-Cold and Wind-Heat pathogens. Wear a scarf in cool air. Avoid sleeping with windows open directly on your face. At the same time, protect against indoor dryness by using a humidifier or placing a bowl of water near heating vents. The skin — the Lung's exterior manifestation — should be kept moisturized with natural oils like sesame or coconut oil.

🍲 Eat White Foods and Moistening Meals (食白润燥)

The color associated with the Metal element and autumn is white. While this does not mean all autumn foods must be white, TCM dietary therapy emphasizes white-colored foods that moisten and nourish the Lung: pears, lily bulbs, white fungus, lotus root, white radish, tofu, almonds, and water chestnuts. Incorporate these into soups, stews, and congees that are naturally warming and easy to digest as the weather cools.

"The Lung is the canopy over the organs. It receives the Qi of heaven, governs the breath, and manifests in the skin. To nourish the Lung in autumn is to fortify the entire body's frontier defense."

— Traditional Chinese Medicine principle

Safety Considerations and Contraindications

While autumn dietary therapy and acupressure are gentle practices for most people, certain precautions apply:

⚠️ Precautions for Autumn Dietary Therapy

  • Dampness and Phlegm patterns: If you have a wet, productive cough, loose stools, bloating, or a thick, greasy tongue coating, avoid excessive sweet, moistening foods (honey, pear, white fungus) — they may worsen Dampness. Focus instead on drying-moistening foods like tangerine peel (陈皮) and white radish.
  • Diabetes and blood sugar concerns: Honey and pear contain natural sugars; consume in moderation and monitor blood glucose. White fungus soup sweetened with rock sugar should be avoided by diabetics — use stevia or simply omit sweetener.
  • Cold Spleen and Stomach: If you experience loose stools, abdominal coldness, or poor digestion after eating cold foods, avoid raw pears and cold white fungus soup. Steam pear with ginger and warming spices instead.
  • Autumn allergies: If you have seasonal allergies to ragweed or mold (common in autumn), be cautious with honey and bee pollen, which may contain trace pollens.

⚠️ Precautions for Autumn Acupressure

  • LU9 (Taiyuan): Located over the radial artery — apply only gentle pressure. Do not use deep pressure or massage devices at this point.
  • LI4 (Hegou): Contraindicated in pregnancy — it strongly moves Qi and may stimulate contractions.
  • Skin integrity: Do not apply acupressure over open wounds, varicose veins, or skin infections on the hands or wrists.
  • Severe respiratory conditions: If you have asthma, COPD, or chronic bronchitis, consult your healthcare provider before using acupressure as a primary treatment. Acupressure is a complementary practice, not a replacement for medical care.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I start autumn wellness practices?

In TCM, autumn begins around Liqiu (立秋) — the "Beginning of Fall" solar term, which typically falls on August 7th or 8th in the lunar calendar. This is earlier than most people expect. Begin transitioning to autumn dietary practices in early August — shift from cooling summer foods to moistening autumn foods, adjust your sleep schedule, and start regular Lung acupressure. By the time the weather visibly cools, your body will already be prepared.

Q: How do I know if I have Lung Yin deficiency?

Common signs of Lung Yin deficiency include: dry throat that is worse at night, dry cough with little or no phlegm (or sticky, difficult-to-expectorate phlegm), hoarse voice, afternoon heat or malar flush, night sweats, dry skin, thirst with a desire for small sips of water, and a red tongue with little or no coating. If you recognize several of these signs, autumn dietary therapy with pears, honey, lily bulb, and white fungus is especially important for you. For severe or persistent symptoms, consult a licensed TCM practitioner.

Q: Can I eat cooling foods like watermelon in autumn?

No — this is a classic TCM dietary error. The Huangdi Neijing explicitly warns: "Do not eat melons in autumn" (秋不食瓜). Watermelon, cucumber, and other cooling summer fruits are too cold for the autumn Spleen and Stomach and can damage digestive Yang. As autumn progresses, shift to neutral or slightly warm moistening foods. If you crave fruit, choose room-temperature Asian pears (warmed with cinnamon and ginger if your digestion is weak), stewed apples, or pomegranates.

Q: Is autumn depression a real concern in TCM?

Yes. As daylight decreases and the air grows colder, some people experience a seasonal mood shift — what Western psychology calls Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD). In TCM, this reflects the Lung's vulnerability to grief and sadness, combined with declining Yang energy. Counter it by: spending time outdoors in morning sunlight, practicing deep breathing exercises, nourishing Lung Yin with moistening foods, stimulating LU7 and LU9 acupoints, and engaging in activities that cultivate gratitude and acceptance — the Metal element's highest emotional expression.

Q: Can children follow autumn dietary therapy?

Yes, and children especially benefit from gentle autumn moistening. The classic "steamed pear with rock冰糖" (冰糖炖雪梨) is a beloved remedy for children's autumn cough. Use small portions — half a pear per day for young children. Avoid honey for children under 12 months due to infant botulism risk. Lily bulb porridge (百合粥) is gentle and nourishing for children with autumn dryness or restless sleep. Always introduce new foods one at a time and watch for allergic reactions.

Q: How does autumn wellness relate to the COVID-19 era of respiratory awareness?

TCM autumn wellness is more relevant than ever in our heightened awareness of respiratory health. While TCM does not claim to prevent or treat COVID-19, the ancient principle of "nourishing the Lung and fortifying Wei Qi" aligns perfectly with modern goals of maintaining robust respiratory immunity. A well-nourished Lung with strong Wei Qi provides the best possible defense against any respiratory pathogen. Autumn dietary therapy and acupressure are gentle, evidence-informed practices that support overall respiratory wellness — a valuable complement to vaccination and standard preventive measures.

Q: What are the best autumn recipes to start with?

Begin with these three simple, powerful recipes:

  • Steamed Asian Pear with Rock Sugar (冰糖炖雪梨): Cut the top off a pear, scoop out the core, fill with rock sugar and a few Sichuan fritillary bulbs (川贝粉), replace the lid, and steam for 30–40 minutes. Eat the pear and drink the juice. Excellent for dry cough and throat dryness.
  • White Fungus and Red Date Soup (银耳红枣羹): Soak dried white fungus overnight, tear into pieces, simmer with red dates, goji berries, and rock sugar for 1–2 hours until gelatinous. Moistens Lung Yin, beautifies skin, and calms the spirit.
  • Lily Bulb and Lotus Seed Porridge (百合莲子粥): Simmer fresh or dried lily bulb with lotus seeds, rice, and rock sugar into a nourishing congee. Calms the mind and stops nighttime cough.
